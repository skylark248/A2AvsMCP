Setup (Slides 1–5)

~7 minutes total

Slide 1 — Title

~30 sec

Open with: "Good morning/afternoon everyone. Over the next 45 minutes or so, I want to walk you through three things that are reshaping how we build AI agents — A2A, MCP, and Skills. Two of these we've covered before in earlier sessions, but A2A is brand new for us today."

Don't dwell on the title slide. Move on quickly.

Slide 2 — Agenda

~1 min

Walk through the four parts, pointing at each numeral as you go:

  • "We have four parts. Section one is A2A — the new piece — and that's where most of our time goes, about 15 minutes."
  • "Section two is MCP, where we did a deep dive in the prior session, so this will be a quick recap — about 7 minutes."
  • "Section three is Skills — also a recap, but with a sharper focus on when to use them versus other things, about 12 minutes."
  • "And section four is where everything composes together — how A2A, MCP, and Skills work as a system in production."

Set expectations: "By the end, my goal is that everyone in this room can look at any agent design problem and know which of these three tools fits."

Slide 3 — Why This Talk

~1.5 min

The key idea: agents alone are isolated and useless in production. Real systems have three integration problems.

Walk through the three boxes:

An AI agent can reason. It can generate text. It can make decisions. But on its own, it's stuck.
In the real world, every agent needs three things: it needs to talk to other agents that someone else built, it needs to talk to your systems — your databases, your APIs — and it needs to know how to do the actual work your business cares about.

Land the punchline: "Three different problems. The industry has produced three different open standards to solve them. That's A2A, MCP, and Skills."

Read the three "By the end" bullets out loud — this is the contract you're making with the audience.

Slide 4 — Quick Vocabulary: 4 Things That Look Similar

~2 min

⚠ This is your densest slide. Walk through it slowly.

Open by acknowledging the confusion: "Before we dive in, I want to clear up something that trips a lot of people up. If you've been using Claude Code or GitHub Copilot, you've probably seen these markdown files — prompt files, instruction files, and now skill files. They all look similar. They do different things."

Lead with the analogy — it's at the top of the slide for a reason:

  • "Picture the agent as an employee. The instruction file is the employee handbook — they always follow it. Doesn't matter what task they're on, the handbook applies."
  • "A prompt file is a form you hand them. They only use it when you give it to them."
  • "And a skill is the SOP binder on the shelf. The employee themselves recognize when a situation calls for it, walks over to the shelf, opens the binder, follows the procedure."

Then move to the table: "The technical distinction is just this — who decides when this content gets loaded? For instruction files, it's always on. For prompt files, you decide. For skills — and this is the key thing — the agent itself decides based on what task it's facing."

Pause and ask: "Does that distinction make sense? Because this is going to come up again when we talk about skills in detail." Take any questions before moving on.

Slide 5 — Three Problems, Three Standards

~1.5 min

This is your framing slide. Make sure it lands.

Walk through the three columns:

  • "Agent talks to another agent — A2A solves this."
  • "Agent talks to its tools and data — that's MCP."
  • "Agent knows your specific way of doing things — that's Skills."

Land the key insight at the bottom: "These are complementary, not competing. A real production agent will typically use all three. We're not picking one. We're learning when to reach for which."

Set up Part 1: "Today, A2A is the new content. MCP and Skills, you've seen before — we'll recap them quickly. Let's start with A2A."

Part 1 — A2A (Slides 6–12)

~15 minutes — your headline section

Slide 6 — Why A2A Exists

~2 min

Open with the story: "Imagine our retail company — and I'll use retail throughout for the recurring examples because it's universal — has built three agents over the past year."

Walk through the three boxes: "One team builds an Inventory Agent in Python. Another team builds a Pricing Agent in a completely different framework. And we have a vendor SaaS providing a Customer Support Agent."

Pose the question: "Each of these works fine on its own. But what happens when inventory drops and pricing needs to adjust? Or when a customer complaint requires checking both inventory and recent pricing changes?"

Land the problem: "Today the answer is custom integration code. Brittle. One-off. Has to be rewritten every time any agent changes. This is the same N-by-M integration problem that drove us to MCP for tools — except now it's between agents."

State A2A's job clearly: "A2A gives agents a standard way to discover each other and coordinate. It doesn't matter who built them or what framework they use."

Slide 7 — How A2A Works: The Agent Card

~2 min

Use the resume metaphor — it's the most accessible explanation:

Every A2A-compatible agent publishes something called an agent card at a known URL. Think of it as the agent's resume. It says: who I am, what I can do, how to reach me.

Walk through the JSON, but don't read it line by line: "What you're looking at is a simplified agent card for a Pricing Agent. It declares its name and description, the URL where it lives, the skills it offers — in this case adjust-price — what input formats it accepts, and how to authenticate."

Walk through the four callouts on the right: "There are four parts to an agent card: Identity — who am I. Capabilities — what can I do, what do I accept as input. Authentication — how do you talk to me securely. Discovery — I'm published at a known URL so other agents can find me without hard-coded integration."

Reinforce the metaphor: "Other agents read this resume before deciding to collaborate. No hard-coded integrations. If a new agent shows up tomorrow with new capabilities, agents that already exist can discover it and start using it. That's the power of the agent card."

Slide 8 — How A2A Works: The Communication

~1.5 min

Set the frame: "Here's where A2A makes a deliberate design choice. It's built entirely on familiar web technology. No new transport, no new protocol invented — just plain HTTP."

Walk through the three boxes:

  • "Transport is plain HTTP. Any web server, API gateway, or load balancer can host an A2A agent. Nothing new here."
  • "The format is JSON-RPC 2.0 — the same format MCP uses. And it's modality-agnostic, meaning agents can exchange text, images, files, structured data — all in the same flow."
  • "And for long-running tasks, A2A uses Server-Sent Events. So if a remote agent is doing something that takes 30 seconds, it can stream partial results back instead of making you wait for the final response."

Land why this matters (read or paraphrase the right-side callout): "Because A2A is built on web infrastructure, agents get all the benefits we already have — routing, load balancing, security, authentication, logging, API gateway policies. We're not building a whole new ops stack."

Slide 9 — A2A vs MCP: The Confusion Buster Pivot slide

~2.5 min

Open by naming the confusion directly: "This is the slide where I want to clear up the biggest source of confusion. Because if you've been paying attention, you may be thinking — wait, A2A uses JSON-RPC over HTTP. MCP also uses JSON-RPC over HTTP. So what's actually different?"

Walk through the five rows of the table, comparing left to right each time:

  • "What they connect: MCP connects an agent to its tools and data. A2A connects an agent to another agent."
  • "What's on the other side: With MCP, it's a passive server — it exposes tools and waits to be called. With A2A, it's another active reasoner — another agent that has its own goals."
  • "The interaction pattern: With MCP, you call a function and you get a result back. With A2A, you send a task — and the other agent might negotiate, might push back, might stream progress over time, might even ask clarifying questions."
  • "Mental model: MCP is the toolbox the agent reaches into. A2A is the peer the agent talks to."
  • "Discovery: With MCP, you as the developer configure which MCP servers your agent connects to. With A2A, agents discover each other through agent cards."
Pause, then land the one-liner — read it out loud, slowly.
If you only remember one thing from this section, remember this: MCP is for capability. A2A is for collaboration.

Optional concrete example: "A database wrapped as an MCP server is passive — it answers when called. A pricing agent reached over A2A is active — it can push back, negotiate, stream partial results, even ask 'what's your budget?' before it acts."

Pause here. Take questions if there are any. This is the pivot slide for the whole talk.

Slide 10 — A2A in Action: Order Fulfillment

~2.5 min

Set the scene: "Let's make this concrete. A high-value order for 50 units of an electronics SKU comes in. Five agents coordinate via A2A to fulfill it."

Walk through each step, pointing at the slide as you go:

  • "Step 1: The Order Agent asks the Inventory Agent — do we have 50 units of SKU-9821?"
  • "Step 2: Inventory responds — only 12 in stock, 38 short."
  • "Step 3: The Order Agent now reaches out to two Supplier Agents — different vendors, different tech stacks, different companies — and asks: can you fulfill 38 units?"
  • "Step 4: The suppliers stream back quotes via SSE as they're calculated. Note: they're not all responding instantly. Some take longer than others, and the Order Agent gets partial results as they come in."
  • "Step 5: The Order Agent picks the best quote, confirms, and notifies the Customer Notification Agent."

Land the punchline at the bottom: "Five agents. Multiple companies. Different tech stacks. All coordinating through one protocol they all understand. No developer wrote custom glue code for any pair of these agents to talk."

Optional add: "This is what 'composability' actually looks like in practice — you can introduce new agents to the ecosystem and existing agents can discover and work with them without code changes."

Slide 11 — Is A2A Real? Should We Bet On It?

~2 min

Open by acknowledging the question: "Now, whenever I introduce something new, the natural question from anyone in this room — especially on the leadership side — is: is this real, or is this hype? Should we actually invest in this?"

Walk through the four bullets:

  • "First — A2A is open. Originally introduced by Google in 2025 as a specification. Not proprietary, not vendor-locked."
  • "Second — same trajectory as MCP. MCP started at one company, Anthropic, and is now industry-wide. A2A is following the same pattern. Multiple vendors are already shipping support."
  • "Third — vendor SDKs are already available. Python, JavaScript, Java, .NET. So whatever stack we're on, there's a path."
  • "Fourth — it's compatible with the major frameworks. LangChain, Semantic Kernel, custom agents, MCP-based agents — A2A doesn't force you to throw anything away."

Land the "For us" callout: "For us — A2A is not ubiquitous yet, but it's clearly heading toward standard. The same vendors that adopted MCP early are now adding A2A support. Worth knowing now so we're not caught off-guard when it shows up in our vendor roadmaps or our own architecture diagrams."

Transition line: "Okay, that's A2A. The new piece. Now let's quickly recap MCP, since most of you have seen this before."

Part 2 — MCP (Slides 13–14)

~5 minutes — keep recap pace

Slide 13 — MCP Recap

~2.5 min

Open with the recap framing: "Most of you have seen this material in our previous two sessions, including the hands-on session where we built an MCP server with FastAPI. So this is a refresher — not new material."

The one-line definition first: "MCP is a standard interface that lets an agent reach tools and data — without rewriting integrations every time you swap a model or framework."

Walk through the four icons:

  • "Architecture: Host plus Client plus Server, communicating in JSON-RPC 2.0."
  • "Primitives: Tools — actions the agent can take. Resources — data the agent can read. Prompts — pre-built templates."
  • "Transport: STDIO when the server runs locally on the same machine, HTTP with Server-Sent Events when the server is remote."
  • "Hands-on: You've built one of these with FastAPI in our prior session. You know what an MCP server looks like from the inside."

Land the key point at the bottom — and emphasize it because it pays off later:

MCP servers are passive. They expose capability. They don't reason. They don't plan. They don't negotiate. That's what distinguishes them from A2A peers.

Connect back to the earlier confusion-buster: "Remember the line from earlier — MCP is for capability, A2A is for collaboration. The passivity of MCP servers is what makes that distinction concrete."

Slide 14 — MCP in Action: Customer Support

~2 min

Set the scene: "Quick concrete example. A customer writes in: 'I want to return the headphones I ordered last month, but I lost the order number.'"

Walk through the four servers:

  • "The support agent reaches the CRM MCP server to look up the customer by email and pull their recent orders."
  • "Then it reaches the Returns MCP server to check eligibility for the matching order."
  • "Then the Logistics MCP server generates the return label."
  • "Finally the Email MCP server sends the label to the customer."

Land the punchline: "Four backend systems. Zero custom integration code in the agent itself. The agent doesn't know about CRM APIs, doesn't know about logistics APIs — it just calls MCP tools. That's the MCP value proposition in one slide."

Optional bridge to Skills: "But here's something worth noticing — MCP gave the agent the ability to call these systems. What told it to call them in this order, with this logic? That's where Skills come in."

Part 3 — Skills (Slides 16–19)

~10 minutes
Transition into this section: "Alright. Skills. Same as MCP, you've seen this before. We'll recap quickly, but I want to focus this section on the question that matters most — when does something deserve to be a skill versus a prompt versus an instruction file? Because that's where confusion still lives."

Slide 16 — Why Skills Exist

~2 min

Open with what LLMs do and don't know: "LLMs are excellent reasoners. They know an enormous amount of general knowledge. What they don't know is how your company specifically does things."

Walk through the three highlighted areas:

  • "Specific procedures — the exact steps for handling a high-value return at our firm."
  • "Periodic workflows — like reconciling inventory across regional warehouses at month-end."
  • "Compliance gates — like the checks that must run before issuing a refund over $10,000."

Set up the dilemma: "Without skills, you have two bad options."

  • "One — you stuff everything into the system prompt or instruction file. But now your context window is bloated for every interaction, even when 99% of those instructions aren't relevant to the current task."
  • "Two — you re-explain the procedure every time. But now the user becomes the SOP document. That doesn't scale."

Land the solution: "Skills solve this by packaging procedures into files the agent loads only when the task calls for them."

Slide 17 — When Is It a Skill? The Decision Tree Pivot slide

~3 min

Frame the slide: "I want to spend extra time on this slide because this is the most common question I get about skills. We have three nearby concepts — instructions, prompts, skills. They look similar. The decision tree on this slide is how you tell them apart."

Walk through the tree top to bottom:

  • "Question one: Is this content used every interaction? If yes — that's not a skill. That's an instruction file. Always-on context."
  • "If no, question two: Does the user explicitly invoke it each time? If yes — that's a prompt file. The user types /refactor or whatever, and the prompt file gets pulled in."
  • "If neither — meaning the agent itself should decide when to use it, based on the task at hand — then it's a skill."

Walk through the two columns at the bottom:

A skill is the right answer when — the procedure has specific steps that must run in order, it applies to a category of tasks (not every task), and the agent should recognize when to use it without being told.
A skill is the wrong answer when — it's a general behavior rule like "always use TypeScript strict mode" — that's an instruction. It's a one-off manual transformation — that's a prompt. It's pure factual lookup — that's RAG. It's a raw API capability — that's MCP.

Use a concrete example to anchor this: "Here's a test. Suppose someone says: 'I want our agent to always reply in formal English.' Is that a skill?

Pause for audience response

— No. That's an instruction. It applies every interaction. Now suppose: 'I want our agent to handle high-value returns following our compliance playbook.' Skill — because it only fires when the task matches, and there are specific steps."

Slide 18 — Skills vs MCP vs RAG

~2 min

Open with the mental model: "The most useful way to remember the difference between these three is this analogy."

Walk through the three columns, pointing at each icon:

  • "MCP is the toolbox. It gives the agent the capability — the actual tools. Like 'I can call create_refund on the API.'"
  • "RAG is the reference manual. It gives the agent facts — retrieved knowledge. Like 'Our return policy says 30 days.'"
  • "Skills are the SOP binder. They give the agent the procedure — the ordered steps. Like 'For returns over $10K: check eligibility, get manager approval, log to compliance, refund, email customer.'"

Land the synthesis line at the bottom — read this out loud:

MCP is the toolbox. RAG is the reference manual. Skills are the SOP that tells the worker which tool to pick up and which page to read first.

One more clarifying point: "A real skill almost always uses MCP tools (to act) and may reference RAG-retrieved policies (to decide). The skill is the orchestration layer — when and in what order."

Slide 19 — Skills in Action: Concrete Examples

~2 min

Set up the table: "Let's make this concrete. Here are scenarios where a skill is the right answer."

Walk through the four rows quickly:

  • "Quarterly inventory reconciliation — same procedure quarterly, specific steps, specific report format."
  • "New vendor onboarding — multi-step, has rules, repeats often."
  • "High-value return needing compliance routing — decision tree the agent should follow, not invent."
  • "Regulatory filing using a standard template — specific format requirements, agent should not improvise."

Look at the YAML at the bottom: "What does a skill actually look like? It's a YAML front matter and a markdown body. This is the high-value-return skill. Name, description — that's the trigger condition. The body contains the actual steps. The agent loads this skill only when the description matches. The other 99% of conversations don't pay the cost of having this in context."

Part 4 — Synthesis (Slides 21–23)

~6 minutes — the payoff
Transition: "Okay, we've covered all three pieces. Now I want to show you how they fit together in a real architecture."

Slide 21 — The Big Picture

~2 min

Frame this as the architecture diagram they should be able to draw: "This is the diagram I want everyone in this room to be able to draw on a whiteboard from memory after this talk. Three agents. Three layers. Three roles."

Walk through the three agents:

  • "Customer Support Agent — owned by one team. It has Skills for handling returns and escalating complaints. It uses MCP to reach the CRM, Orders DB, Email server, Returns API."
  • "Inventory Agent — owned by a different team. Different Skills, different MCP servers."
  • "Supplier Agent — external vendor. We don't even see inside it. All we know is it speaks A2A."

Land the three roles at the bottom:

  • "A2A is how agents coordinate with their peers. The vertical arrows between agents."
  • "MCP is how each agent reaches its tools and data. Into the agent."
  • "Skills is the procedural knowledge inside each agent — what tells it what to do."

Optional: "Notice that the Supplier Agent is opaque to us. That's the power of A2A — we don't need to know how the external agent works internally. We just need to know its agent card and the protocol."

Slide 22 — End-to-End: All Three Pieces Pivot slide

~3 min

This is the slide that makes everything click. Spend real time here.

Frame it: "This is the slide that makes everything click. One scenario — a high-value return request comes in. Let's watch all three pieces fire."

Walk through each row, naming the piece each time:

  • "Step 1: Customer message arrives. Agent recognizes 'return over $10K' — and loads the handle-high-value-return skill. The skill's description matched."
  • "Step 2: The skill instructs the agent to pull customer and order details. It calls the CRM and Orders MCP servers."
  • "Step 3: The skill says — check inventory before approving the refund. The agent calls the Inventory Agent over A2A."
    Slow down here — this is the A2A moment in the workflow.
  • "Step 4: Skill says route for manager approval. MCP call to the approval server."
  • "Step 5: Skill says log to compliance. MCP call to the audit server."
  • "Step 6: Skill says notify customer. MCP call to email."
One agent. One skill. Several MCP tools. One A2A peer. That's the shape of a real production agent workflow.

Optional emphasis: "Notice the skill is the orchestrator. It's the brain of this whole flow. MCP and A2A are the muscles. Without the skill, the agent wouldn't know what to do. Without MCP and A2A, the skill couldn't actually act on anything."

Slide 23 — Decision Cheat Sheet

~1 min

Tell them to capture this slide: "This is the slide to photograph. If you take away one reference from this presentation, this is it."

Read through the cheat sheet quickly:

  • "Two or more agents to coordinate? Reach for A2A."
  • "An agent to read/write data or call APIs? MCP."
  • "An agent to follow a specific multi-step procedure? Skill."
  • "Agent to know a fact? RAG."
  • "Always-on behavior rules? Instruction file."
  • "User-invoked templates? Prompt file."

Land the common production pattern: "In real production, you'll use multiple of these at once. A2A for coordination, MCP for capability, Skills for know-how, instruction files for project-wide rules. They're not competing. You compose them."

Closing (Slides 24–25)

~2 minutes before Q&A opens

Slide 24 — What This Means For Our Team

~2 min

Open by translating to action: "So what does all of this mean for us, the Portfolio Accounting Tech team? Three concrete takeaways."

  1. MCP servers are our integration leverage: "Every internal system we wrap as an MCP server — position DB, ledger, trade store, regulatory reporting — becomes available to every future agent we build. Write once, reuse forever. This is the cheapest, highest-leverage thing we can do right now."
  2. Skills encode our institutional knowledge: "The procedures our team has refined over years — month-end recon, position break investigation, regulatory filing prep — these are exactly what skills are for. We can make them reviewable, version-controlled, and reusable across every agent."
  3. A2A is the bet for the next 12 months: "As more teams across the firm build agents, A2A is what lets them compose without us writing custom integration code per pair. Even if we don't adopt it tomorrow, it's worth tracking."

Close with the open standards line: "And the good news — all three are open standards. What we build today carries forward. We're not betting on any single vendor."

Anticipated Q&A — Quick Reference

Have a 30-second answer ready for each

"Is anyone at Morgan Stanley actually using A2A yet?"

"Not widely yet — it's still early. The protocol was introduced in 2025. But we're seeing vendor support grow fast, and the same vendors that drove MCP adoption are now adding A2A. The right answer is to be ready, not to be first."

"How is this different from microservices?"

"Microservices follow deterministic logic. They do what you coded them to do. A2A peers are AI agents that reason — they can negotiate, push back, ask clarifying questions, adapt. It's a different abstraction layer."

"Why not just use REST APIs between agents?"

"You could. But A2A adds two things REST doesn't: standardized discovery via agent cards, and standardized task semantics — including streaming progress, modality handling, and negotiation. You'd end up reinventing all of these per integration."

"Won't this proliferate complexity?"

"Yes — if used badly. The cheat sheet on Slide 23 is the antidote. Most projects don't need all three. Match the tool to the problem."

"What about RAG?"

"RAG is the fact-lookup layer — we covered it briefly on the Skills vs MCP vs RAG slide. RAG retrieves relevant information; skills tell the agent how to use it. They're complementary, but RAG wasn't the focus of today's talk."

"How do we secure agent-to-agent communication?"

"A2A inherits standard web security. OAuth2 is supported out of the box, as is API key authentication. Because it runs over HTTP, all our existing security infrastructure — gateways, WAF, monitoring — applies."

"Which one should we pilot first?"

"I'd suggest MCP — wrap one internal system as an MCP server. It's the lowest-risk, highest-leverage starting point. Then build a small skill on top of it. A2A becomes relevant once we have multiple agents to coordinate."

Final Delivery Tips

Read once before going on stage
1
Don't rush. 40-45 minutes is plenty of time. Pause after key slides — especially Slide 9 (A2A vs MCP confusion-buster), Slide 17 (Skills decision tree), and Slide 22 (end-to-end).
2
Repeat the one-liners. "MCP is for capability. A2A is for collaboration." Say it twice if you can — once entering Slide 9, once leaving it. Same for "MCP is the toolbox, RAG is the reference manual, Skills are the SOP."
3
Use the analogies. Employees with handbooks, forms, and SOP binders. Toolbox / reference manual / SOP. These are sticky. They'll be remembered.
4
Walk slowly through Slide 22. This is the synthesis moment. Don't blow through it. Each row should be its own beat — "Step 1, skill. Step 2, MCP. Step 3 — and here's where A2A enters the workflow." Let it land.
5
Stay conversational. Don't read these notes. Internalize them, then talk like you would to a colleague at lunch. Your audience will engage more if it feels like a conversation, not a lecture.
6
Be honest about uncertainty. If someone asks something you don't know, say so. "I'd have to dig into that — let me follow up after." That builds more trust than improvising.
Good luck. You're well prepared. The deck is solid, the structure is right, and the message is clear. Focus on connecting with the audience and the rest will follow.
